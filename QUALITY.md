# Quality ledger

This file is the single place where the corpus's known defects, open re-sourcing work, and quality history live. A book is either **clean** (no entry here), **flagged** (listed below, with the same caveat stated in its own `source_note`), or **resolved** (moved to the log at the bottom). If you find a defect that is not listed here, that is a bug in the ledger as much as in the book: please open a content-correction issue.

## How quality is enforced

| Gate | Tool | When |
|---|---|---|
| Rendering-risk lint (14 pattern classes) | `scripts/lint-corpus.py` | CI, every push and PR |
| Machine-index freshness | `scripts/build-manifest.py --check` | CI |
| Verse hard-break detection | `scripts/check-verse-breaks.py` | Contributor-run; flags are leads that need human judgment |
| Upstream fidelity audit (Wikisource books) | `scripts/verify-upstream.py` | Contributor-run per book |
| Full structural render audit | An external renderer, outside this repository | Maintainer, at merge |
| Full-corpus critical read | Native-reader audit passes | Periodic; last: 2026-07-20 |
| Documented-counts consistency | `scripts/check-doc-counts.py` | CI, every push and PR |

## How to audit a book

The mechanical gates above catch structure; they cannot catch a wrong word. That takes a reader, and it is the most valuable contribution a fluent speaker can make here. The method below was distilled from the 2026-07 full-corpus audit wave that repaired more than sixty books:

1. **Read the file next to its source.** Open the book's `source_url` (for scan-backed sources, the page images) beside the markdown. You are comparing against the cited edition, not against the text you know from school: many books here deliberately preserve historical orthography.
2. **Know the defect classes.** Nearly everything the audit waves found falls into seven bins: duplicated or dropped passages (conversion seams, page boundaries); mis-encoded characters (mojibake, OCR confusions like cl/el or rn/m, lost diacritics); lost structure (missing chapter headings, flattened section numbers); soft-wrapped verse rendering as prose (run `scripts/check-verse-breaks.py`); transcriber or OCR debris (page numbers, running heads, apparatus) left in the text; silent modernization (orthography that does not match the cited edition); and wrong attribution or edition metadata in the frontmatter.
3. **Sweep mechanically first.** Before the read-through, search the file for markup residue (`{{`, `</`, `&#`), stray Latin letters inside non-Latin text (or vice versa), and improbable character runs. A few minutes of searching will surface things a long read-through can skim past.
4. **Sample honestly, or read fully.** A full read-through is best. Where the book is long, read the opening, the ending, and at least two randomly chosen interior sections; defects cluster at conversion seams (chapter boundaries, page turns), so check several of those too.
5. **Record what you find, precisely.** File a content-correction issue (or PR) per defect class, quoting the file line and the source reading. "Chapter 7 heading missing; the source has ROZDZIAL VII on p. 214" is actionable; "some OCR errors" is not.
6. **Fixes must be honest.** Every correction beyond pure transcription repair gets named in the file's `source_note`; silent fixes are bugs, per the corpus's editorial method. Update this ledger (Open flags in or out, Resolved log entry) in the same PR as the fix.
7. **Excise by provenance rather than by legibility.** When cutting debris from a damaged text, cut only what the printing house did not produce. Copy-specific accidents (library stamps, date-due cards, bookplates, owners' marginalia, hand-lettered replacement leaves, scan boilerplate) may be excised with a named disclosure. Everything set as part of the edition (indexes, errata slips, printer's advertisements, tables) stays, however badly the OCR mangled it: a garbled index still witnesses that the edition had one. Worked example: the chr *Constitution* entry below.

A worked example of the full cycle is the gd *Moladh Beinn-Dòrain* entry in the Resolved log: better scan located, systematic OCR classes fixed wholesale, full read-through, named corrections disclosed, ledger updated.

### la: Vergil, *Aeneis*
- **Defect**: the file carries two orthographic conventions. Liber I writes consonantal v (avena, silvis, venit); Libri II-XII write u (renouare, uidi, Auerno). Both are inherited exactly as la.wikisource has them, and neither matches the Greenough 1900 print edition the page names, which uses v throughout and prints no macrons. The macron layer itself is the same kind of addition: it comes from The Latin Library, whose Aeneid I is macronised and uses v, and whose Books 2-12 are unmacronised and also use v. So the macrons on Liber I arrive by the cited route, while both the macronisation and the u-normalisation of Libri II-XII are a Wikisource layer with no stated base. No text is lost or garbled, and the poem reads correctly under either convention. The problem is that a single work carries two of them.
- **Path**: normalise to a single convention, which means resolving consonantal from vocalic u word by word rather than by regex (uult, suus, and tenuis all defeat a naive rule). A Latin reader could do Libri II-XII to v in an afternoon, or the whole file to u. Either direction is defensible; v matches the cited edition, u matches eleven twelfths of the current file.
- **Done means**: one convention across all twelve books, the choice and its direction stated in source_note, and the macron layer's provenance restated to match whatever base the normalisation follows.

## Conventions this ledger assumes

- `source_note` is the per-file honesty channel: OCR tier, named corrections, edition caveats. Silent fixes are bugs.
- The discovery layer (`title_translit` / `title_en` / `author_translit`) exists on every non-Latin-title book.
- `manifest.json` is the machine index; regenerate after any book change.
- Anthologies of works by many hands credit the composers rather than the collector (see mi *Ko Nga Moteatea*, ta *Kuruntokai*).
- A structural division's title appears exactly once, as its markdown heading; duplicated in-body title lines are removed with disclosure, and the chapter-head wording wins over a table of contents where they differ (FRONTMATTER.md §Headings and chapter titles).
